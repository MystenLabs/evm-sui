TL;DR

Production EVM development converges on a small canon. Tokens + access control dominate by every measured proxy; factory/clone deployment is the dominant operational pattern (>90% of deployments); proxies are everywhere but almost never actually upgraded (98.24% of upgradeable proxies never upgraded once). The security pattern canon is stable: checks-effects-interactions, pull-payments, reentrancy guards, authorization checks.

The official sui-for-ethereum page already maps the top three (access control → capabilities, token standards → Currency Standard, proxy upgrades → package upgrades). The mid-tier use cases — staking/vesting, escrow, payment splitting, multisig, merkle airdrops, permit/meta-transactions, flash loans — are entirely absent from it. That is chapter 2's opportunity space.

Key findings (all confirmed 2-1 or better)

1. Tokens and access control are the two pillars HIGH

OpenZeppelin Contracts accounts for 155,000+ verified deployments across Ethereum and major L2s. Its per-library ranking is a direct prevalence signal: Tokens 150k+, Utilities 150k+, Access Control 115k+, Proxies & Upgradability 75k+, Governance 1k+. Independent factory-clustering research (ACM TOSEM, Jan 2026) corroborates: 63.6% of the top 3,000 verified factory contracts are "Finance & token", vs 15.4% "Proxy & upgrade" and only 2.6% "NFT & creator". Caveat: OZ figures are self-reported and library counts overlap.

2. Proxies are pervasive — but for cloning, not upgrading HIGH

Two peer-reviewed studies put proxy prevalence between 3.25% (bytecode detection, 2M of 62.6M contracts, ICSE 2025) and 14.2% (delegatecall-trace analysis, EMSE 2024) of all Ethereum contracts. The killer detail: 80.46% of proxies are upgradeable in capability, but 98.24% of those have never been upgraded once. 67.8% of proxies act as plain interceptors/forwarders; 79% derive from known reference implementations; ERC-1167 minimal clones alone are 29.4%. Production Ethereum uses proxies mostly as a cheap code-sharing mechanism, not a live upgrade mechanism.

Chapter 2 implication: don't frame the Sui counterpart as "upgrades" only. The honest mapping is twofold: (a) code-sharing/cloning → Sui needs no clones at all (one package, many objects); (b) upgrades → native, UpgradeCap-mediated, layout-compatible package upgrades — no delegatecall, no storage-collision hazard.

3. Factories mint >90% of all contracts HIGH

Longitudinal measurement (434M contract runtimes disassembled): factory contracts have minted over 90% of all contracts deployed on Ethereum and Polygon since 2020, concentrated among few deployers (top 11 deployers = 50% of live Ethereum contracts). Factory+clone is the mass-deployment idiom — think Uniswap pair factories, Safe proxy factories, NFT drop minters. Caveat: measures raw deployment volume, not per-developer adoption.

4. The upgradeability design space is standardized HIGH

The community converged on delegatecall proxies by ~end of 2020. Eleven named variants exist in production (Inherited Storage, Eternal Storage, EIP-1967 Unstructured Storage, UUPS/EIP-1822, Transparent, EIP-2535 Diamonds, Beacon, Registry, EIP-1167, EIP-897, EIP-1538-withdrawn), mostly documented via OZ blogs or EIPs. The canonical constraint — "only append new variables to the storage" — and its storage-collision hazard are exactly the pain points Sui's layout-compatibility check absorbs into the language.

5. Micro-patterns are near-universal HIGH

Across 23,258 verified contracts on five chains, 99% implement at least one of 18 micro-patterns (avg 2.76 per contract). Only five exceed 40% coverage: storage packing (84.62%), event-less code (54.75%), delegation (48.73%), interface implementation (46.33%), external library use (45.20%). In the literature, control patterns dominate (51%) over security (24%), performance (20%), maintainability (5%).

Caveats, refuted claims & open questions

Caveats on the data

  • Proxy statistics snapshot Sept 2022 (EMSE) and Sept 2023 (ICSE); micro-pattern data runs through Dec 2024; OZ impact figures are undated and self-reported ("OpenZeppelin's own reported figure").
  • Proxy prevalence is strongly methodology-dependent (3.25% bytecode lower bound vs 14.2% trace-based vs 54.2% of active contracts in a third study) — always report the range.
  • Factory ">90%" measures raw automated deployment volume, not per-developer pattern adoption.
  • All verified-contract datasets share selection bias — unverified contracts are invisible to them.
  • DeFi TVL category breakdowns and Solmate/Solady prevalence were requested but did not survive verification — the "which building blocks dominate" answer rests on OZ data alone.

Refuted in verification (do not cite)

  • REFUTED "The sui-for-ethereum page omits staking/vesting/escrow/multisig/oracles/governance/airdrops/permit" — refuted 1-2 because the page does carry infra-level rows for oracles/governance. Superseded by the direct section-by-section audit above.
  • REFUTED OpenZeppelin's "$220B TVL / 88% market share" marketing figure — refuted 1-2, do not use.

Open questions

  • Relative prevalence within Tier 3 (Safe deployments vs vesting contracts vs merkle distributors vs Chainlink consumers) — no verified quantitative data emerged.
  • Solmate/Solady market share vs OpenZeppelin, and whether gas-optimized idioms shift what the snippets should mirror.
  • How DeFi TVL categories (DEX/AMM, lending, liquid staking) map onto contract-pattern prevalence.
